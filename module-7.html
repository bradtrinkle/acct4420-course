Step 3 · Learn · 1 hr

Sections 3a to 3h are reading and quick checks (3h ends with one question for you). The tax you need is short on purpose: your job is to get AI to do the drafting, checking, and calculating, check it, and decide. You apply every technique yourself in Step 5.

3a. A research memo, and what counts as a source

Tax professionals answer a question in a research memo with five parts: the question; the facts (what happened); the rules (the authorities that apply); the analysis (applying the rules to the facts); and the conclusion with a next step. The rules part is where sources matter. An authority is a source anyone can open and read: the Internal Revenue Code (the federal tax law), Treasury regulations, IRS notices, and court decisions. IRS publications are helpful guidance, but a tax professional prefers the notice or regulation behind them. A citation is the address of an authority. Articles by accounting firms are helpful commentary, but a tax professional relies on the authority itself, because commentary can be out of date or wrong.

3b. AI and citations: a right answer you cannot check is not a citation

AI writes text that sounds right. With citations, three things can go wrong: it can cite a real rule with the wrong section number, cite a rule that has since changed, or cite something that does not exist. Often an AI memo is mostly right and still cannot be relied on, because you cannot tell which parts are right without opening the sources. Asking "are you sure?" does not fix this, because AI will sound sure either way. What does help is to give the AI the sources, make it quote them, and then open the ones that matter yourself.

3c. Give AI the sources: source-grounded prompting

When you attach the sources (here, the Source Packet) and tell Claude to use only those sources, to quote the exact words and the label of each source for every rule it relies on, and to write "not in the sources" when the sources do not answer, you can check every statement against text you hold. The "not in the sources" rule matters most: it turns a gap into a visible flag instead of an invented fill-in.

Technique: Source-grounded prompting (also called grounding, or open-book prompting; it is the idea behind retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, where AI answers from documents it is given)

Why it helps: Answers can be checked against text you can see, and gaps show up as "not in the sources" instead of guesses. In Step 5 you write your own template that does this.

3d. Have AI list its sources, then check them: citation tables and cite-checks

Ask Claude for a citation table: one row per source with the citation, what it says that source supports, and the exact words relied on. Then ask Claude to check each row against your Source Packet and mark it Supported (the packet says it), Different (the packet says something else; say how), or Not in the packet (the packet cannot confirm it). "Not in the packet" does not mean wrong. It means a person must open the original or leave it out. ("Not in the packet" is the mark you put in a table; "not in the sources" says the same thing in a sentence.) If your Claude has web search, you can also ask it to look for the official text behind a "Not in the packet" row, quote the page's own words, and give the web address. Treat that as a lead: a person still opens the page.

Technique: Structured extraction of claims, then verification prompting (also called self-verification or a cite-check: the AI compares its own statements against the sources)

Why it helps: AI is good at comparing text against text, and the table turns a wall of prose into a list you can check line by line. But AI checking AI can still be wrong, so a person opens the two or three sources that matter most.

3e. Let AI calculate with code, and show its work

AI counts and calculates best when it uses code (you used this in Module 6). The same goes for tax math: ask Claude to use code, to show each item's calculation, and to state the rate and rule it used for each line. Then recompute one item yourself in a spreadsheet. When a fact is missing (such as a contract date), ask for both outcomes instead of a guess.

A worked example of the two-part first-year deduction (made-up item, not Velox's): an item that cost $100 and was acquired under the older rule gets a 40 percent deduction first, which is $40.00. The remaining $60 is then deducted over several years, and its first-year part is 14.29 percent of $60, which is $8.57. The item's total first-year deduction is $48.57. An item acquired under the newer rule gets all $100 in the first year.
