Step 3 · Learn · 50 min

Sections 3a to 3g are reading and quick checks (3g ends with one question for you). The accounting you need is in 3a and 3b and is short on purpose: your job is to get AI to do the calculating, check it, and decide. You apply every technique yourself in Step 5.

3a. Which costs matter for a decision?

The question is: will Velox be better off with the order than without it? To answer it, compare what the order adds (the money received) with what it adds in cost. Only costs that change because of the decision count. Accountants call these relevant costs. A cost that is the same whether or not Velox takes the order is not relevant.

  • Variable costs rise with each extra car: materials, assembly labor, delivery freight, the warranty reserve. They are relevant.
  • Fixed overhead is the plant's costs that do not change with a few more cars: supervisors, depreciation on equipment, utilities. Reports often spread it over the cars built (this is called allocating it), so each car seems to carry, for example, 44,600 of it. But the plant pays the same total either way, so it is not relevant to this decision.
Your turn

The cost sheet shows 44,600 of fixed overhead per car. Should it count against the special order?

3b. What each car adds, and how many cars fit

Take the price of a car and subtract the variable costs of building and delivering it. What is left is the contribution margin: what each extra car adds toward the plant's fixed costs and profit. If the contribution margin is positive, each car helps. Two more things change the answer:

  • Capacity. Cars built inside unused regular-time capacity cost only the variable costs. Cars beyond that need overtime, which adds an extra cost per car. So the number of cars that fit in regular time matters.
  • Side effects. Slower payment ties up cash, which has a cost. And a low price offered to one customer can leak and make regular customers ask for lower prices too. These are part of the real cost of the order even though they are not on the cost sheet.

The cost of waiting for money. If a customer owes you $100,000 and pays 60 days later than usual, and you borrow at 8 percent a year to cover the wait, the wait costs 100,000 × 8% × 60 ÷ 365 = about $1,315. (Use 365 days in a year.)

Break-even means the point where the order adds exactly nothing, for example the lowest price per car at which the order is worth accepting. In this module, find it before counting the dealer-price risk, and show it both before and after the cost of the extra payment days. AI can find it for you.

Inputs a special order needs: the number of cars; the price per car; the variable cost per car; the cars that fit in regular time; the overtime limit and the overtime extra cost per car; the extra cost of any rush parts and how many cars need them; the payment days and the borrowing rate; and the size of the price-leak risk. You will use a list like this to write your template in Step 5.

3c. Let AI do the work: a real example

Here is a made-up decision at Prairie Tool & Die, a small Velox supplier (not Velox itself, so this is not your answer). Prairie has been offered a special order of 1,000 steel brackets at $38.00 each, due this month. Its regular price is $52.00. Each bracket costs $22 in materials, $6 in labor, and $3 in variable overhead to build ($31 in all), plus $9 of allocated fixed overhead that does not change. Regular customers buy about 4,300 brackets a month, so the plant's fixed overhead is $38,700 a month ($9 × 4,300). Regular time has room for 700 more brackets; the other 300 would need overtime at $5 extra each. A manager worries regular customers may ask for a 2% price cut if they learn of the $38 price. We gave Claude these facts and asked it to explain three options and three cases. This is Claude's real output, condensed:

OptionHow Claude worked it outResult
1. Accept as offered ($38)700 regular-time brackets: ($38 − $31) × 700 = $4,900. 300 overtime brackets: ($38 − $36) × 300 = $600.+$5,500
2. DeclineNo sale, so no added revenue or cost.$0
3. Counter at $41 for all 1,000700 × ($41 − $31) = $7,000. 300 × ($41 − $36) = $1,500. If the customer refuses, the result is $0.+$8,500 if accepted
Case (accepting as offered)What happensResult
Best and baseRegular customers never ask for a cut. Claude said the files give no basis for a better outcome, so best and base are the same.+$5,500
WorstRegular customers get a 2% cut: $52 × 2% = $1.04 on all 4,300 regular brackets = $4,472 lost.$5,500 − $4,472 = +$1,028

What to notice: (1) Claude kept the $9 of fixed overhead out and explained that counting it would make the order look like a $2 loss per bracket, which is misleading because the $9 does not change. (2) For every number it gave the formula, the numbers it used, and a check. (3) It also found the break-even price cut (about 2.5% of the regular price) and listed what is not in the files, such as how likely a leak is and whether the customer would accept $41. It did not invent a better best case. Your job is to get this kind of answer for Velox, and then to check it.

3d. Write the request once: a template with defined inputs

The next special order will have different numbers but the same questions. Instead of retyping a request each time, write it once as a template: a request with named blanks for the things that change. Each blank is an input with a name in square brackets, a unit, and a note on where to find it. Here is a short example for a different job, checking a month's travel expenses:

Check these travel expenses against the policy. Inputs: [EMPLOYEE NAME] (text, from the expense report) [TOTAL CLAIMED] (dollars, from the expense report total line) [DAILY LIMIT] (dollars per day, from the travel policy) [TRIP DAYS] (number of days, from the trip dates) Show the formula, the numbers used, and the result. List anything you could not check. A person must confirm the total before it is paid.

Technique: Prompt template with defined inputs (also called a parameterized prompt: a request with variables)

Why it helps: The next time, you only change the inputs, so the request does not get shorter or sloppier. Defining each input (name, unit, source) also tells you exactly which numbers to look up and check. In Step 5 you write your own template for special orders.

Your turn

What is the main benefit of marking [TOTAL CLAIMED] with its unit and where to find it?

3e. Ask "what if": best, base, and worst

A single answer hides the risk. Ask AI to run the decision under different assumptions: a base case (the facts as given), a best case, and a worst case. Then ask where the answer flips: for example, how big a price cut by regular customers would erase the profit. If the files do not support a different case, AI should say so, as Claude did in the example. And have AI list its assumptions and what is not in the data.

Technique: Scenario analysis and sensitivity analysis (what-if prompting, finding the break-even point), together with listing assumptions

Why it helps: A decision maker needs to know not just the expected result but how wrong it could go and what would change the answer. AI can run many what-ifs in seconds, and you can check one or two of them by hand.

Your turn

AI says accepting adds 5,500 in the base case and 1,028 in the worst case. What does the worst case tell the manager?

3f. AI analyzes. You decide.

AI can calculate options and explain their results. It cannot decide, because it does not own the consequences. When you recommend, write it so that the reader can act on it: start with the recommendation (accept, decline, or counter-offer on stated terms), give the few numbers that drive it, say what conditions or events would change your mind, and list what you do not know. A person signs the decision; the AI's work is an input.

Step 4 · Ethics checkpoint: bias · 30 min

JR
JordanIf Velox accepts the order, about 70 cars would be built on overtime, and someone has to decide who works that overtime. That is exactly the kind of job where AI can quietly treat people unfairly. Area 3 of the Velox Responsible AI Policy says: Bias. Ask whether the data or the AI's answer could treat a group unfairly, and test for it. Judge the five suggestions below the way a fair-minded manager would.
The rule: a plan that decides who gets an opportunity must not depend on age, health, medical or family leave, or any other personal trait, or on a stand-in for one (a proxy: a measure that quietly stands in for something you must not use, such as attendance records standing in for health), and it must not rely on data that leaves a group out. A rule set by the labor agreement, such as seniority, is allowed. The plan must also be checked by a person for patterns in who actually gets the opportunity: reviewing the results by group is how a person checks for bias; it does not mean using the trait to decide. A named person must be accountable for the plan, never the AI. You still follow the whole policy (its five areas are confidentiality and privacy, accuracy, bias, transparency and disclosure, and human accountability); area 3 is the focus of this step and of Part 7, and the policy link above is optional reading.

How to choose a verdict: choose Meets the policy only if the suggestion does not depend on a personal trait or a proxy for one, does not rely on data that leaves a group out, and a person checks the results for patterns.
