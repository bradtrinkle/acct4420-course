Words you need

A bank reconciliation compares two records of the same cash: Velox's own books (called the book balance) and the bank's statement (the bank balance). The two numbers usually differ for ordinary reasons. You find each reason, adjust whichever balance needs it, and show that both sides end at the same adjusted balance (the balance for that side after you apply its adjustments). Here are the usual reasons:

ItemWhat it isWhich balance you adjust
Deposit in transitVelox recorded a deposit, but the bank had not yet added it by month-end.Bank balance goes up
Outstanding checkVelox wrote and recorded a check that has not yet cleared the bank.Bank balance goes down
Bank errorThe bank made a mistake, such as charging Velox for another customer's check.Bank balance goes up if the bank wrongly took money out, down if it wrongly added money
Bank service chargeA fee the bank took that Velox has not recorded yet.Book balance goes down
Interest earnedInterest the bank paid that Velox has not recorded yet.Book balance goes up
NSF checkA customer's check Velox deposited bounced ("not sufficient funds"), so the bank took the money back.Book balance goes down
Electronic deposit not yet recordedA customer paid electronically straight into the bank, and Velox has not recorded it yet.Book balance goes up
Book errorVelox recorded the wrong amount (for example two digits swapped).Book balance goes down if Velox recorded a payment too small (or a receipt too large), up if the reverse

More words: A reconciling item is any difference between the two records that you find and explain. ACH is an electronic bank-to-bank payment, a wire is a same-day electronic transfer, a dealer remittance is a payment from a car dealer, and CSV is a plain spreadsheet file you can open in Excel.

Rule of thumb: timing items (Velox knows, the bank does not yet) change the bank balance. Items only the bank has recorded change the book balance. An error is fixed on the side that made the mistake.

Step 3 · Learn · 45 min

Sections 3a to 3g are reading and quick checks (3g ends with one question for you). You apply every technique yourself in Step 5.

3a. Why one big request is risky

A reconciliation has many small steps: line up two lists, spot the lines that do not match, decide what kind of difference each one is, adjust the right balance, and add it all up. If you ask for all of it in one request, the answer arrives as a finished-looking whole. When something is wrong, you cannot see which step went wrong, and AI may fill a gap with something that merely fits.

Your turn

Why is a single request for a whole reconciliation risky?

3b. Break the job into steps

Split the job into steps, run one step at a time, check each result, and feed the good result into the next step. Each step is its own request, and each one is small enough for you to check. For example, suppose you had to review a batch of vendor invoices for duplicate payments. The steps could be: list the invoices, find pairs that look the same, decide which pairs are real duplicates and why, then summarize what to recover. Notice that nothing here is reconciliation. In Step 5 you will write your own request for each step of Velox's bank reconciliation.

Technique: Task decomposition, carried out as prompt chaining (a chain of prompts where each answer feeds the next)

Why it helps: Each step is small enough for you to check, so mistakes are caught early instead of being buried in the final answer. It also lets you steer: if step two looks wrong, you fix it before step three builds on it.

Your turn

You are asking AI to review 200 vendor invoices for duplicates. Which is the best way to start a chain?

3c. Ask for the reasoning and the assumptions

When AI makes a decision, such as "this unmatched line is an outstanding check," ask it to say why, and to name which line it relied on and any assumption it made (for example, "I assumed the check was written before month-end"). You are not looking for a magic phrase. You are making the thinking visible so that you can find the weak spot. Many newer AI models reason internally before they answer, but the reasoning you can see may be partial or missing, so ask for the reasons in the answer itself.

Technique: Chain-of-thought prompting (asking AI to show its reasoning), together with stating assumptions

Why it helps: A visible reason is something you can check against the source. A hidden reason is something you can only trust. Named assumptions show you exactly where a human judgment is needed.

Your turn

AI classifies an item and says only "outstanding check." What is the most useful follow-up?

3d. Make AI challenge its own answer

After AI finishes, ask it to act as a skeptical reviewer of its own work: look for items it missed, counted twice, put on the wrong side, or numbers that do not add up. This finds slips a first pass missed. But AI can also look at its own answer and say "all good" when it is not, so a self-review never replaces your own check.

Technique: Self-critique (also called red-team prompting or self-refinement: asking AI to look for flaws in its own work)

Why it helps: A second pass with a different job ("find what is wrong") often catches things the first pass ("do the task") did not. It is cheap, and it tells you where to look first.

Your turn

AI reviews its own reconciliation and replies that everything is correct. What should you conclude?

3e. Run it twice and compare

AI answers can vary from one run to the next. So run the same task a second time in a brand new chat (so it cannot see the first answer) and compare. Where the two runs agree, you can have a little more confidence. Where they differ, you have found exactly the items to check first. Agreement is not proof, because both runs can make the same mistake.

Technique: Self-consistency (running the same task independently more than once and comparing the answers)

Why it helps: Differences between runs point you straight to the shaky items, and agreement raises your confidence a little. It is a fast way to decide where your checking time should go.

Your turn

Two independent runs give you the same list of reconciling items. What does that tell you?

3f. Check the result against the source

AI is fluent, but it can slip on arithmetic and on matching lines. For a reconciliation, you check three things yourself: (1) recompute the totals in Excel or Google Sheets and see whether the two adjusted balances really agree; (2) trace each reconciling item to a specific line in the source files (date, reference, and amount); and (3) look for what is missing or invented: items AI left out, and any adjustment that has no source line. An unexplained adjustment added only to make the two sides agree is called a plug. A plug is a red flag in any reconciliation.

Technique: Verification against the source (recompute, trace to the source, and use a second tool such as a spreadsheet to check the first)

Why it helps: It turns "this looks right" into "I confirmed it." A spreadsheet adds up numbers exactly, and a source line is evidence, so each check catches a different kind of error.

3g. Save the recipe

When you have a chain of prompts that works, save it as a template with square brackets where the details change, for example [MONTH] and [ACCOUNT NAME]. Next month you reuse the recipe instead of starting from nothing, and so can Walt's team.

Technique: Reusable prompt template (a saved prompt chain with blanks to fill in)

Why it helps: You get the same quality every month, and a teammate can run the process without you. In Step 5 you will save your own version as this week's reusable piece.

Source: what the records say

  • Balance per bank statement: 412,500.00. Balance per books: 398,640.00.
  • Deposit recorded on July 31 that the bank has not yet credited: 24,000.00.
  • Checks recorded but not yet cleared: #3017 for 9,800.00, #3022 for 6,250.00, and #3025 for 2,310.00.
  • On the bank statement but not in the books: bank service charge 85.00, interest earned 210.00, and an electronic payment received from Granite Ridge Motors 19,375.00.

The AI's work

  • Claim 1. "A deposit in transit of 24,000.00 is added to the bank balance."
  • Claim 2. "The three outstanding checks total 19,360.00."
  • Claim 3. "The bank service charge of 85.00 reduces the book balance, and the interest of 210.00 increases it."
  • Claim 4. "A 'miscellaneous adjustment' of 19,375.00 is added to the book balance."
  • Claim 5. "The adjusted bank balance is 412,500.00 + 24,000.00 − 19,360.00 = 417,140.00. The adjusted book balance is 418,140.00. The 1,000.00 gap is just rounding."

Helpful: adjusted bank balance = bank balance + deposits in transit − outstanding checks. Adjusted book balance = book balance − charges + interest + any electronic receipts not yet recorded. Check each claim against the source and with your own arithmetic.

How to choose a verdict: choose Supported by the source when everything in the claim is true. Choose Wrong or not supported by the source when the claim contains any incorrect number or a conclusion the source does not support, even if some figures in it are right. Choose The number is supported, but the explanation is missing or wrong when the amount really exists in the source but the AI gave it a wrong label (such as a plug) or did not say what it is. If any figure or direction is wrong, choose Wrong or not supported by the source; choose the third option only when the number and direction are right and only the label or explanation is wrong. Judge each claim including any figure it relies on.
