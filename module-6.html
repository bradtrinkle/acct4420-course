Step 3 · Learn · 50 min

Sections 3a to 3h are reading and quick checks (3h ends with one question for you). The auditing you need is in 3a and 3b and is short on purpose: your job is to get AI to do the extracting and counting, check it, and report carefully. You apply every technique yourself in Step 5.

3a. Indicators are leads, not proof

One common kind of fraud is a fake vendor: a company that exists only on paper, set up so that payments go to the person who created it. Auditors look for signs, which they call indicators (some people say "red flags"). Examples: invoices that sit just under an approval limit, so no second person has to approve; several invoices on the same day that together would pass the limit; payments with no purchase order (you can see this only in the invoice records, not in the ledger); urgent "pay today" requests; a vendor set up recently; or payments approved by the same person who set the vendor up.

The rule that matters most: an indicator is not proof. Some clues are also weak: two account numbers that match only in their last four digits are a small match, because many unrelated accounts end in the same four digits. Each indicator has innocent explanations, and fraud is decided by people who gather documents and hear from the people involved, not by a pattern in a spreadsheet. Wrongly accusing someone harms a real person. So your job is to find the leads, test them, and report them honestly.

3b. Benford's Law: a way to test thousands of amounts

In many real sets of money amounts (payments, invoices, expenses), the first digit is not equally likely to be 1 through 9. About 30 percent of amounts start with 1, and the share falls steadily to under 5 percent for 9. This pattern is called Benford's Law. Here is the expected share for each first digit:

First digit123456789
Expected share30.1%17.6%12.5%9.7%7.9%6.7%5.8%5.1%4.6%

Why auditors like it: people who invent amounts, or keep them just under a limit, usually do not follow the pattern. A big gap between the expected and the actual share of a digit is a reason to look closer. A gap is not proof either. Honest data breaks the pattern too, for example a vendor paid the same fixed fee every month (every payment starts with the same digit), or amounts that stay in a narrow range. The test also needs a lot of amounts (hundreds or more) to be meaningful. The practical rule: count first digits, find the digits that are off by about 2 percentage points or more (too high or too low), then find which vendors cause each gap.

3d. Turn messy records into a table: structured extraction

Records arrive as emails, scans, and forms, written in different ways. To count or compare anything, first put the facts in a table. Ask AI to do it, and tell it exactly what table you want: the columns (each with a name and a format, such as a date as YYYY-MM-DD), what to write when a fact is missing ("not stated", never a guess), and to quote the words it used for the key fact so you can check it in seconds.

Technique: Structured extraction (also called schema-based or information extraction: you give the AI a fixed set of fields to fill from messy text)

Why it helps: It turns text you cannot sort or count into a table you can. The "not stated" rule keeps AI from guessing, and the quoted words let you spot-check any row. In Step 5 you write your own extraction template.

3e. Let AI count with code

Counting first digits across two thousand rows by reading them is exactly what AI does badly: it can sound sure and be off by dozens. Instead, ask Claude to use code on the attached file (Claude has a built-in tool for this; on paid plans it is a setting you can switch on). Code counts exactly. You then check one or two of the counts yourself in a spreadsheet.

Technique: Tool use, specifically code execution (asking the AI to run a program on your file instead of estimating by reading)

Why it helps: Counting and totaling by code is exact and repeatable, and the code can be shown to you. AI that "eyeballs" a large file can be confidently wrong.

3f. Ask for the innocent explanations

When AI flags something, ask it the opposite question too: "What are the most likely innocent explanations for each flag, and what would I need to see to check them?" A good answer, like Claude's in 3c, gives a reason a person can test. This keeps you from treating every flag as guilt and keeps the report fair.

Technique: Alternative-explanations prompting (a form of devil's-advocate or red-team prompting: asking the AI to argue against its own flag)

Why it helps: The first answer from AI tends to follow the question's framing. Asking for the other side shows you which flags are likely harmless and which need a person's attention.

Your turn

3g. Report indicators for a person to act on

An audit report says what the data show, how strong each lead is, what is not known, and what a person should do next. It refers to people only by ID, never accuses anyone, and never says "committed fraud." Start with the most important sentence: what the data show and that they are indicators for follow-up. Then give the few numbers that matter, the innocent explanations you considered, what you do not know, and the next step for a person (such as getting documents). The person who owns the investigation decides what happens next.
