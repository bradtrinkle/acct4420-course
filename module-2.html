Step 3 · Learn · 45 min

Steps 3a to 3f are reading and quick checks. You apply every technique yourself in Step 5.

3a. Why the first answer felt generic

AI cannot see your situation. When you leave details out, it fills the gaps with a guess about the average case. The more relevant context you give, the less it has to guess. That context is the difference between a generic answer and a useful one.

Your turn

Imagine you typed a request like "Write a summary of Q3 results" and the answer was generic. Why?

3b. The five parts of a clear request

Most good requests have five parts. You do not need fancy wording, just clear answers to these five questions. (In this module, "part" in Step 3 means one of these five. The assignment sections in Step 5 are called "Part 1" to "Part 8" and are a different thing.)

PartThe question it answersExample
RoleWho should AI act as?"You are a quality analyst at an auto manufacturer."
GoalWhat should it produce, and why?"Draft a one-page summary of last month's warranty claims so the plant manager can decide where to add inspections."
AudienceWho will read it?"A plant manager with ten minutes between meetings."
FormatWhat shape should it take?"A short paragraph, then a table of the top three causes."
ConstraintsWhat rules must it follow?"Use only the numbers I attach. Stay under 250 words."

These examples are for a different task (a warranty-claims report for a plant manager). You will write your own five parts for Walt's summary in Step 5.

Technique: Structured prompting (the five-part request)

Why it helps: It removes the guessing. Each part answers a question AI would otherwise have to guess, so the first draft is closer to what you need and you spend less time fixing it.

Technique: Role prompting (the Role part)

Why it helps: Telling AI who it is acting as sets its vocabulary, level of detail, and point of view, so the answer sounds like the person you would hire for the job.

Technique: Structured output (the Format part)

Why it helps: Asking for a specific shape, such as a headline and three bullets or a table, makes the answer easy to scan and easy to check, and keeps every run consistent.

Your turn

Here is a request: "You are a plant analyst. Summarize the attached downtime log for the plant manager, who reads for two minutes. Use hours, not minutes, and no speculation." Which of the five parts is missing?

3c. Ground it in your own data

Give AI the facts it should use (attach them or paste them), and tell it, in your own words, two things: to rely only on those facts, and what to do when the facts do not explain something (say so instead of guessing). This matters because, as you learned in Module 1, AI can fill a gap with something made up (a hallucination). A grounding sentence gives it a safe way to say "I don't know."

Technique: Grounding (also called source-grounded prompting)

Why it helps: AI answers from the facts you gave it instead of from memory. That cuts invented numbers and reasons, and it makes every claim checkable against a source you can point to.

Your turn

Which instruction best keeps AI from making things up?

3d. Show an example

One good example teaches style faster than a paragraph of rules. Attach or paste a past summary, then tell AI, in your own words, to copy its style and structure but to take every number only from the new data. The second half matters, because AI can copy the example's numbers by mistake.

Technique: Few-shot prompting (giving AI examples; with exactly one example it is called one-shot)

Why it helps: An example shows tone, length, and layout faster than any list of rules. It is the quickest way to make AI match your team's house style.

Your turn

You paste last quarter's summary as an example. What should you add to your request?

3e. Let AI ask you questions first (clarifying questions)

Add one line, in your own words, asking AI to put a few questions to you (up to three works well) before it writes anything, and to wait for your answers. AI will often ask about things you forgot, such as who signs the summary or what threshold counts as material. You are still in charge: you decide how to answer.

Technique: Clarifying questions (also called interview-style prompting: letting AI ask before it answers)

Why it helps: AI finds the gaps in your request before it writes, so you fix them once instead of redoing the whole draft. It is also a good habit for you: if AI needs the answer, a human reader probably does too.

Your turn

What is the main benefit of letting AI ask questions first?

3f. Check the answer against the source (a check, not a prompt)

A clear request raises the odds of a good answer. It does not guarantee one. Pick three specific claims in the output (numbers or reasons) and find each one in your data. A reason that is not in your data is unsupported, no matter how reasonable it sounds. Remove it, or confirm it with the person who knows.

Technique: Verification against the source (checking claims, sometimes called fact-checking the output). It is something you do after any prompt technique.

Why it helps: Tracing three claims to the data catches the errors that matter before they reach an executive.

Step 4 · Ethics checkpoint: confidentiality · 30 min

JR
JordanThe more context you give AI, the better the answer. The risk is that context can include things that should never leave Velox. That is area 1 of the Velox Responsible AI Policy: Confidentiality and privacy. Good judgment means giving AI what it needs and nothing more.
The rule: never put confidential, personal, or nonpublic data (information the company has not released to the public) into an AI tool unless it is approved for that data, and anonymize first (remove or replace anything that identifies a person, customer, or supplier). In this course, treat your own Claude account as a tool Velox has not approved for nonpublic information.

Open the full policy in a new tab (optional: it lists all five policy areas; this module covers area 1 only)

Velox and its numbers are fictional and cleared for this course, so the budget numbers you used in Step 2 are fine to use. Pretend the eight notes below are real. At a real company, even the numbers would need approval before going into an AI tool.

Walt's real file has an internal_notes column next to the variance numbers (you will download it in Step 5, Part 2). Below are eight notes from it, each with the row it sits in. For each one, choose what to do before using the data with AI:

  • Keep: use it as written. It is needed for the summary and does not expose anyone or anything sensitive.
  • Generalize: replace it with general words. For example, change a named customer to "a large dealer group".
  • Remove: leave it out completely.

Ask yourself two questions: Does the summary need it? and Would it cause harm if it leaked? There can be more than one defensible answer. Give a reason for each. In your reasons, describe a note in general words (for example, "the bonus note"); do not copy the sensitive details, because Step 5 sends your draft to Claude.
