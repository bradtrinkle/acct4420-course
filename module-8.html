WordWhat it means here
Month-end closeThe set of checks a finance team runs after a month ends, to make sure the books are right before reporting them.
Bank statementThe bank's record of every deposit and withdrawal in the company's account this month (what the bank says happened).
Cash ledger (GL cash)The company's own record of the same account (what the books say happened). GL stands for general ledger, the main book of accounts.
ReconcileCompare the bank statement with the cash ledger item by item and explain every difference, so that both end at the same true cash balance.
Variance reportA table of budget versus actual for each line of the income statement, with the difference (the variance) and a short internal note. Amounts are in thousands of dollars ("300" means $300,000).
Payment registerThe list of payments the company made to its suppliers this month: who was paid, which invoice, how much, and who approved it.
Approved Vendor ListThe list of suppliers the company has approved to be paid.
Adjusted (true) cash balanceThe cash balance both sides agree on after every difference between the bank statement and the cash ledger is explained. Each side starts from its own ending balance and ends at this one number.
Deposit in transit, outstanding checkNormal timing differences: a deposit in transit is in the books but not yet on the bank statement; an outstanding check is written in the books but not yet cashed.
NSF (returned) itemA customer's payment that the bank returned because the customer's account lacked funds ("not sufficient funds"). The deposit is taken back.
SubtotalA line that adds or subtracts other lines (on the variance report: Gross profit, Total operating expenses, and Operating income).
ExceptionSomething that does not look right and needs a person to look at it.

3a. A workflow is a fixed path; an agent chooses its own

In Step 2, Claude chose what to check, what thresholds to use, and when to stop. That is flexible, and it is also why two runs can differ. A workflow is the opposite: you fix the steps, the order, and the places where a person must look, and the AI does the work inside each step. An agent (Module 9) is an AI that chooses its own steps. For a task that must be the same every month and that someone will sign, start with a workflow: it is predictable, and you can test it.

3b. Chain the stages: each stage feeds the next

Your machine runs four stages, each doing one job and handing a short, clear result to the next: Stage 1 reconcile cash (the bank statement against the cash ledger, ending in one true cash balance); Stage 2 explain the variances (the variance report); Stage 3 scan the payments (the payment register and the vendor list); Stage 4 write the close memo from what the first three found. Each stage reuses a piece you already made: your Module 3 reconciliation chain for Stage 1, your Module 2 variance template and Module 4 "numbers say what, notes say why" habit for Stage 2, your Module 6 payment checks and Module 7 "not in the sources" rule for Stage 3, and your Module 2 prompt constraints, Module 5 template with defined inputs, and Module 7 write-for-the-reader habit for Stage 4. The rules (thresholds, what counts as an exception) do not go inside the stages; Walt keeps them in one rules file, so he can change a rule without anyone rewriting the machine.

Technique: Prompt chaining (also called a multi-step workflow or a pipeline: a task split into stages, where each stage's output is the next stage's input)

Why it helps: Each stage is small enough to check, a mistake is easier to locate, and the same stages run the same way every month. In Step 5 you write the stages into your own instructions.

3c. Gates: where a person must stop the machine

Your machine has two gates. Gate 1 comes after Stage 1, because the adjusted cash balance feeds the memo and a wrong number there spoils everything after it. Gate 2 comes after Stage 3, just before the memo is written, because a person should decide which exceptions go in front of the Controller. At a gate the machine writes "GATE 1" or "GATE 2" and stops and waits. You then read, and approve, correct, or ask a question. A gate works only if the person actually reads. A gate approved without reading is a rubber stamp, not a control. Also remember that "nothing found" is not the same as "looked and found nothing": at a gate, check that the machine says what it tested, not only what it found.

Technique: Human-in-the-loop checkpoints (also called gates, approval steps, or human review)

Why it helps: The AI does the heavy lifting; a person keeps the judgment and the accountability. The checkpoint sits where an error would be most costly or hardest to see later.

3d. Instructions that make it a machine

The machine lives in a Claude Project (you built one in Module 4). Three things go in it. The rules file and the Approved Vendor List are knowledge files (Claude reads them every run). The instructions say how to work: which files it uses, the four stages and what each must produce, where to stop, how to write the memo. And every month you attach the monthly files to a new chat. Good instructions also include guardrails: use only the files and write "not in the data" instead of guessing; name every item by its code exactly as it appears in the files; use code for every calculation and show the formula; stop and tell the person if a file is missing or a required column is missing; and never say or imply that anyone committed fraud or name an individual.

Technique: Standing instructions plus knowledge files (a system prompt with attached documents), tool use for calculations (code execution), and guardrails (a stop-if-something-is-wrong rule, sometimes called failing safe)

Why it helps: The same rules and procedure apply to every run, whoever starts it. A machine that stops when something is wrong is safer than one that improvises.

3e. Test it like a product: caught, missed, and false alarms

How do you know the machine works? Do not judge it by how confident its memo sounds. Test it on a month where you know the answers. Walt plants problems in each month's files. You count how many your machine caught (found), how many it missed, and how many false alarms it raised (it flagged something that was fine). Practitioners call caught-out-of-planted recall, and flagged-items-that-were-real precision. A machine that flags everything has perfect recall and is useless, because a person must then check everything. You want both numbers high. Walt's scorer combines them in one number: problems caught, divided by (problems planted + false alarms). Missing a problem and raising a false alarm each pull it down.

Use three kinds of month: a practice month (October) where you may see what you missed and fix your machine; a hidden test month (November) that you run once, as Walt's Gauntlet; and a fresh month (December) to see whether your fixes work on something new. This matters because of one rule: fix the rule, not the practice month. If the machine missed a duplicate payment, the fix is a general instruction ("test every payment against every other for the same vendor, invoice number, and amount"), never a note about the particular payment. A machine tuned to one month's answers can look perfect there and fail on the next one. Practitioners call that overfitting. And after you change anything, re-run an earlier month to check that you did not break what used to work (regression testing).

Technique: Evaluation against a test set (often called evals): known problems, caught versus missed versus false alarms (recall and precision), a held-out test month, and regression testing after changes

Why it helps: It replaces "it sounds right" with a number you can compare from one version of the machine to the next, and it shows whether a fix works in general or only on the month you used.

For this demonstration we told the machine to treat both gates as approved so you could see all four stages in one place. You will not do that: you will stop and read at each gate.

What to notice: (1) With a file missing, the machine stopped and invented nothing. (2) Every total was calculated with code, with the formula and inputs shown, and the two adjusted balances agree to the cent. (3) It applied Walt's rules, not its own: the bank's amount was assumed right, and the thresholds came from the rules file. (4) It said what the note did not explain and put that in the memo as an exception, instead of accepting a note that did not fit. (5) The memo uses codes only, names nobody, says "not in the data" where the files are silent, and ends with a line for a person to sign. (6) The machine is a very fast first pass, not the last word: a person still opens the files behind each exception.
