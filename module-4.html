Words you need

Companies report three financial statements. Each answers a different question:

StatementThe question it answers
Income statementHow did the company do over a period of time? It lists revenue (money from selling cars), the costs, and the profit that is left.
Balance sheetWhat does the company own (assets) and owe (liabilities) on one particular day? What is left for the owners is equity.
Cash flow statementWhere did the company's cash come from and where did it go during the period? Profit and cash are not the same thing.

More words: Cost of goods sold is what it cost to build the cars that were sold. Gross profit is revenue minus cost of goods sold. Operating income is profit from running the business, before interest and taxes. Net income is the final profit after interest and taxes. Current assets are things Velox expects to turn into cash within a year (cash, money customers owe, inventory); current liabilities are bills due within a year. Depreciation is the cost of equipment spread over its life; it lowers profit but no cash leaves the bank that quarter. Accrued liabilities are bills Velox owes but has not paid yet. Severance is pay to employees whose jobs end, and a restructuring charge is the cost of reorganizing a business. A tariff is a tax on imported goods. Freight-out is the cost of shipping cars to customers. Capital expenditures are money spent on long-lived equipment such as a paint line. Dividends are cash paid to shareholders. Earnings per share (EPS) is net income divided by the number of shares. The effective tax rate is income tax divided by income before taxes. Q3 2026 means the three months ended September 30, 2026, and Q3 2025 is the same three months a year earlier. All amounts are in thousands of dollars, so 338,000 means $338 million.

Step 3 · Learn · 1 hr

Sections 3a to 3g are reading and quick checks (3g ends with one question for you). You apply every technique yourself in Step 5.

3a. Three statements, three questions

In Step 2, AI could describe the income statement but could not say where the cash went, because that is a different statement. To explain a quarter you need all three: the income statement (how we did), the balance sheet (what we own and owe), and the cash flow statement (where the cash came from and went). This week the balance sheet compares June 30 (the start of the quarter) with September 30 (the end), so that it connects to the cash flow statement for the same three months. You also get management notes: facts from the Controller's Office that explain why some numbers moved. The statements tell you what happened; only the notes tell you why.

How to read the cash flow statement: a plus number brings cash in and a minus number takes cash out. "Increase in inventory: -18,000" means Velox spent 18,000 of cash on more inventory, so cash went down even though inventory (an asset) went up. Depreciation shows as a plus because it reduced profit but did not use any cash.

3b. Ratios turn numbers into a story

A ratio divides one number by another so that you can compare things of different sizes, such as this quarter against last year. You will have AI calculate these six this week:

RatioFormulaWhat it tells you
1. Gross marginGross profit ÷ revenueHow many cents of each sales dollar are left after building the cars
2. Operating marginOperating income ÷ revenueHow many cents of each sales dollar are left after all operating costs
3. Current ratioTotal current assets ÷ total current liabilitiesWhether Velox can pay its bills due within a year (above 1 means yes, with room to spare)
4. Debt-to-equity(Current portion of long-term debt + long-term debt) ÷ total shareholders' equityHow much Velox borrows compared with what the owners have put in
5. Days inventory on handEnding inventory ÷ cost of goods sold × 92 (the days in the quarter)How many days of sales are sitting unsold in inventory
6. Cash conversionNet cash from operating activities ÷ net incomeHow much of the profit actually turned into cash

Ratios 1 and 2 compare Q3 2026 with Q3 2025. Ratios 3 and 4 compare September 30 with June 30. Ratios 5 and 6 are for Q3 2026 only, and the management notes give a target for each.

3c. Give AI the facts once: a Claude Project

In Step 2 you pasted data into a single chat. That works once, but it does not scale: you would paste the statements again every time, and you could forget a rule. A Claude Project (you built one in Module 1 for Jordan) is a workspace that keeps two things for every chat you start inside it: standing instructions that you write once, and knowledge files that Claude can read each time. Good standing instructions for reporting work cover seven things:

  1. Role: who AI is acting as.
  2. Audience: who will read the results.
  3. Output format: the shape of the answer.
  4. Grounding rule: use only the files, and write "not in the data" when the files do not say.
  5. Show-the-calculation rule: give the formula and the numbers used for every calculation.
  6. Confidentiality rule: what must not appear in anything for people outside finance.
  7. Disclosure line: the line that goes on every deliverable saying AI helped and who reviewed it. Leave the reviewer's name as a blank, like [reviewer name], for a person to fill in only after that person has actually reviewed the work.

Technique: Project instructions (standing or persistent instructions, also called a system prompt or custom instructions), together with knowledge files (grounding AI in your own documents)

Why it helps: You set the rules and the facts once, and every chat in the project starts from the same place. That makes results more consistent, saves retyping, and keeps your rules from being forgotten. In Step 5 you save your own instructions as this week's reusable piece.

3d. Make AI show the calculation

Ratios are arithmetic, and AI can slip on arithmetic or pick the wrong number from a statement. So ask for the formula, the numbers it used, and the result for each calculation. Then you can check each number against the statement in a few seconds. Some Claude plans can also run code to do the arithmetic exactly. If yours does, you still check that it used the right inputs, because a correct calculation on the wrong number is still wrong.

Technique: Show-your-work prompting (chain-of-thought applied to calculations, with the formula and inputs listed)

Why it helps: A result alone can only be trusted. A result with its formula and inputs can be checked line by line, so errors are easy to see.

3e. Say who it is for

The same numbers need different words for different readers. Priya has two minutes and needs the bottom line and what is still unknown. An investor analyst wants specifics. Employees need plain language and nothing confidential. When you tell AI who the reader is, how long they have, and what shape the answer should take, the first draft lands much closer.

Technique: Audience-aware prompting (also called tone or register control: telling AI who the reader is and how they read)

Why it helps: The reader decides what to include, what to leave out, and how technical to be. Saying so up front saves you from rewriting a draft written for the wrong person.

3f. Numbers say what; notes say why; everything else is "not in the data"

You met grounding in Module 2: use only the facts you were given. It matters most when explaining results, because AI is very good at inventing a believable reason. For Velox, a cause is real only if the management notes say so. If a line moved and neither the statements nor the notes explain it, the honest answer is "not in the data", plus who could tell you.
